Have you ever heard of biang biang noodles Do you know how the name come out In Xi’an, the skill of noodle-pulling is so closely connected with the slapping (拍击) sound that the special noise ended up becoming this dish’s funny name: biang biang noodles.
The “biang” character is onomatopoeic (拟声的). It is like the sound of hitting a board. It is also believed to be more difficult to write than any other character in the Chinese language, with 58 strokes (笔画). It is surprising to discover that the character can’t be found in dictionaries. That’s because the character is completely a folk creation. After further understanding of the symbol, I realized that it is also made up of many different elements (元素). Together, they paint a picture of Xi’an’s rich history.
Hiding in biang, for example, is the Chinese character for silk. Xi’an was the starting point of the Silk Road. Along those trade routes, horses were not only used for the transport, but were traded as a products. It makes sense, and then, the biang character includes the symbol for a horse. On both sides of the horse symbol, the Chinese character meaning long or length is also added. Whether this is translated to the Silk Road or to the noodles, both would seem appropriate.
It’s unclear exactly how the difficult character was created. One widely told story says that it was created by a young teacher from the Qin Dynasty who didn’t have enough money for a bowl of biang biang noodles and offered to invent a character instead of payment. Today, there are interesting poems to help remember all of the difficult character’s strokes.

Dear Xi’an Volunteer Center,
I’d love to be a volunteer in the Old People’s Home or Children’s Hospital, helping the people who are in need of help.
I want to be a volunteer because I enjoy helping others and making our city a better place. I have some experience. For example, I helped my grandma at home every week, so I am good with old people. Also, I am patient and kind. I am interested in playing games and telling stories, which might cheer up sick children. My plan is to spend my Saturday mornings helping there. I can talk with the elderly, read books to them, or play simple games with the children.
I’m looking forward to your reply.
Yours,
Li Hua
